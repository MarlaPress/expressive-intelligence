# Stop Letting Your Body Apologize for Your Message

You can say the right words and still weaken the message.

You can say you are excited while your face looks flat. You can talk about something painful while your expression barely changes. You can make a bold offer while your shoulders round, your weight shifts backward, or your voice suddenly gets smaller.

That is what I call **Body Apology**.

Your words are making the statement. Your body is saying, “Sorry.”

And audiences notice, even when they cannot tell you exactly what feels off.

Your body should not decorate your message. It should agree with it. And your body language should not be an afterthought. It should help the thought land.

## Your body is already talking

A lot of speakers treat body language like an accessory. First they write the talk. Then they practice the words. Then, somewhere near the end, they think about where to stand or what to do with their hands.

That is backwards.

Your body is communicating from the moment people see you. Before you say a word, your posture says something. Your face says something. Your movement says something. Your use of space says something.

The question is whether all of it supports the message or quietly fights against it.

If you say, “I know this works,” while shifting your weight backward and looking uncertain, the audience gets two messages. If you say, “I am thrilled to be here,” while your face barely moves, they get two messages. If you say, “This changed my life,” while your body stays rigid and disconnected, they get two messages.

And when words and body disagree, the audience feels the contradiction.

## Body Apology shows up in small ways

Body Apology is rarely dramatic. Most of the time it is subtle.

A speaker makes a strong statement, then physically backs away from it. They round their shoulders, shift weight from one foot to the other, step backward, drop their chin, fold inward, or start pacing because standing still suddenly feels uncomfortable.

The words may be bold. The body is asking permission.

That is Body Apology.

And it gets even more noticeable when you make an offer.

You can describe your service confidently for twenty minutes, then suddenly look smaller when you tell people how to work with you. Your voice changes. Your body retreats. Your energy drops.

That physical shift says more than the offer itself.

If you do not look certain about what you are offering, why should anyone else feel certain about it?

## Own the space you are in

A lot of speakers barely move. They find one spot and stay there like someone glued their shoes to the carpet.

Others do the opposite. They pace constantly with no connection between the movement and the message.

Neither one helps.

Movement should have meaning.

Step forward when the point gets more personal or direct. Move to another part of the stage when the conversation changes. Give yourself room to expand when the idea gets bigger.

You do not need choreography. You need intention.

Owning the stage does not mean dominating it. It means you look like you belong there.

## Zoom makes congruence even more important

On Zoom, people get less of you. They see your face, your shoulders, and maybe part of your upper body. That is it.

You have fewer physical tools available, which means the ones they can see matter more. Your face becomes a much bigger part of the message.

If you are excited, show it. If you are talking about something sad, difficult, frustrating, or meaningful, let your face reflect that too.

You do not need to perform emotion. You need to stop hiding it.

A lot of people become strangely neutral on Zoom. They stare into the camera with the same expression whether they are talking about a breakthrough, a tragedy, or what they had for lunch.

That flatness costs connection because facial expression gives the audience emotional context. Without it, the words have to work much harder.

## Your hands should mean something

I am a big believer in using your hands when you speak, especially on video. But using your hands is not the same as waving them around.

Some people make the same gesture over and over again. Hands open. Hands close. Hands open. Hands close.

It becomes visual wallpaper. The movement does not mean anything.

Instead, let your hands reinforce the thought.

If you say there are three things, hold up three fingers. If you are talking about something personal or emotional, touching your chest can reinforce that the message is coming from you.

If you are talking about the past and future, use space intentionally. You might gesture to one side for the past and the other for the future.

If you are talking about something growing, expanding, or getting larger, let your hands show expansion. If you are narrowing the conversation to one key idea, bring your hands closer together.

Your body language should not be an afterthought. It should help the thought land.

That is the difference between gestures that are decorative and gestures that actually communicate.

## Smile when the smile belongs there

A lot of speaking advice tells people to smile more. Sometimes that is good advice.

I often recommend smiling at the beginning of a talk because it can create warmth and rapport quickly. I also like a smile at the end, especially after you make an offer, if it fits the moment.

But smiling all the time can actually make you less congruent.

If you are talking about something painful while smiling, the audience gets confused. If you are describing a serious challenge with a cheerful expression, something feels wrong.

Your facial expression should move with the message.

That is not performance. That is emotional congruence.

## Body image can become Body Apology too

This is a smaller piece of the problem, but it matters.

When people do not like the way they look, they often start adjusting their body in ways they do not even notice. They turn sideways too often, round their shoulders, stoop, become stiff, try to hide parts of themselves, or reduce their movement.

They are not thinking, “I am going to make myself smaller now.” But that is what happens.

Self-consciousness changes physical expression.

You do not have to love every angle of yourself to speak powerfully. But you do need to stop letting discomfort about your body dictate how much space you allow yourself to take up.

## Do not let your body apologize after the talk either

This is one of my favorite Body Apology moments because it happens after the speaker thinks the talk is over.

They finish. The audience applauds. And the speaker immediately turns and runs off the stage.

Or they start clapping along with the audience.

Why? They do not know what to do with the attention, so they escape it.

But the applause is still part of the communication.

Stay there. Look at the audience. Let yourself receive it. Smile if that feels natural. Take in the moment.

You do not need to milk the applause. You just need to stop rejecting it.

Running away can quietly say:

“I do not deserve this.”  
“I am uncomfortable being seen.”  
“I need to get out of here before you change your mind.”

Your talk is not finished until your body leaves the stage.

## Powerful body language is not about looking powerful

This distinction matters.

I do not want you walking around thinking:

Shoulders back.  
Hands here.  
Stand like this.  
Smile now.

That can become another performance.

The goal is not to manufacture body language that looks confident. The goal is congruence.

If the words are strong, let the body support them. If the moment is emotional, let the body reflect it. If the idea is expansive, let yourself expand. If the point is personal, let yourself come closer to it.

That is very different from posing.

- whether your face matches the emotion
- whether your hands support the words or distract from them

## Your body helps the audience understand

When body language is congruent, it does more than make you look confident. It helps the audience process the message.

A gesture can make an abstract idea visual. A facial expression can tell the audience how emotionally important something is. A pause combined with stillness can make a sentence feel heavier. A step forward can signal that something matters.

That is why body language should never be treated like the extra thing you add after the talk is written.

Your body language should not be an afterthought. It should help the thought land.

## Start noticing the apologies

The easiest way to improve this is not to memorize a list of gestures. Start by noticing your Body Apologies.

Watch yourself on video.

Do you step backward when making a strong statement? Do your shoulders round when you talk about yourself? Does your face stay flat when your words are emotional? Do you use the same meaningless hand motion twenty times?

Do you suddenly shrink when making the offer? Do you rush away from applause?

Do not judge it. Notice it.

Then ask:

**What would congruence look like here?**

That one question changes everything.

Your body is part of your message whether you intend it to be or not. **Body Apology** happens when your posture, face, movement, gestures, or energy weaken or contradict the words you are saying.

On stage, that may look like shifting backward, rounding your shoulders, pacing without purpose, or shrinking when you make an offer. On Zoom, facial expression and meaningful upper-body gestures matter even more because the audience sees so little of you.
