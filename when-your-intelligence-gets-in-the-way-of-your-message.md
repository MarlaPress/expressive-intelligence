# When Your Intelligence Gets in the Way of Your Message

Some of the smartest people I know are surprisingly hard to listen to. Not because they have nothing valuable to say. Usually it is the opposite. They know so much that they cannot decide what to leave out.

Ask them a simple question and you get the history, the exceptions, the background, three caveats, a side story, and somewhere around minute seven, the answer.

They think all that detail proves how much they know. Sometimes it does. It can also make the message harder to find.

I call this **Smart Stuff**.

Smart Stuff is what happens when you know so much that you stuff the message with more information than the audience needs. The information may all be good. It may all be accurate. It may even be fascinating.

But if the audience has to dig through all of it to find the point, your intelligence is getting in the way of your message.

## Smart people have a particular communication problem

When you know a subject deeply, almost everything feels relevant. You know why the answer is not quite that simple. You know the exception someone might bring up. You know what happened before the thing you are talking about. You know the research, the nuance, the competing opinion, and the story behind the story.

Your audience does not. More importantly, they usually do not need to.

This is where smart speakers get into trouble. They communicate based on everything they know instead of what the audience needs to understand right now.

Those are two very different things.

The expert is thinking, “But there is more to it.” The audience is thinking, “What is the point?”

## More information does not automatically create more credibility

A lot of people unconsciously believe that sounding smart means showing their work. So they qualify everything.

“I think this could potentially be useful in certain situations depending on…”

By the time they reach the end of the sentence, the original thought has lost most of its force.

There is a place for nuance. There is a place for qualifications. There are situations where precision matters enormously. But when every statement comes wrapped in explanation, clarification, and another little disclaimer, the audience has to work too hard to figure out what you actually believe.

You can know an issue is complicated and still say something clearly about it. In fact, that is often one of the signs that you really understand it.

## Smart Stuff buries the point

Think about packing a suitcase for a three day trip.

You start with what you need. Then you add something “just in case.” Then another option. Then a second pair of shoes. Then one more jacket because the weather might change.

Pretty soon, you can barely close the suitcase.

That is Smart Stuff.

The original idea might be strong:

**People trust clarity.**

Then the speaker adds context, a caveat, another example, an explanation of the example, and a disclaimer so nobody misunderstands the explanation of the example.

The point is still in there. You just buried it.

And sometimes Smart Stuff turns into something I have written about before, **Worthsplaining**.

You are no longer explaining because the audience needs more information. You are explaining because some part of you wants to make absolutely sure they understand how much you know.

## Clear does not mean simplistic

This is where some experts resist. They hear “simplify” and think, “You want me to dumb it down.”

No.

Clear is not dumb. Simple is not shallow.

You can talk about sophisticated ideas without making the listener work like they are assembling furniture with half the instructions missing.

The real skill is deciding what they need first. Give them the spine of the idea before you hand them every bone in the body.

Start with the point. Explain what is necessary for them to understand it, then stop.

You can always go deeper when the audience needs deeper.

## Your audience should not have to prove they are smart enough to follow you

Experts often become so comfortable with the language of their industry that they forget what normal conversation sounds like. They use terminology their colleagues understand. They stack abstract concepts on top of one another. They speak in sentences that might work perfectly in a white paper but sound unnatural out loud.

Then when the audience gets lost, the speaker assumes the audience just does not understand the subject.

That is backwards.

Communication is not a test of whether the audience can reach you. It is your job to reach them.

That does not mean stripping the intelligence out of your message. It means making your intelligence usable.

## Sometimes the problem is not what you know. It is where you are speaking from.

There is another reason intelligent speakers can be harder to follow.

They stay almost entirely in their heads while they speak. They are thinking about the next point, checking whether the previous sentence was technically accurate, monitoring the audience, remembering the statistic, deciding whether they should add the caveat, and editing themselves while words are already coming out.

That is a crowded place to speak from. It can make someone sound knowledgeable but disconnected.

This is where **homespace** matters.

I teach people to speak from more than their head. Bring in the heart. Bring in the gut. Bring in what you actually believe and feel about what you are saying.

Your expertise gives the message substance. Your full expression gives it life.

You need both.

## Watch for the urge to prove

One of the easiest ways to catch Smart Stuff is to notice when you start trying to prove something.

Prove you are qualified. Prove you have done your homework. Prove there are no holes in your argument. Prove you have thought of every possible objection before someone else can mention it.

That urge changes the way you communicate. You stop serving the idea and start defending yourself.

The audience may never consciously identify it, but they feel the extra effort.

This is where Expressive Intelligence™ becomes useful.

E-IQ helps you notice what is underneath the extra words. Is the audience confused and asking for more? Great. Explain more.

Or are you uncomfortable letting a clear statement stand without immediately softening, qualifying, or proving it?

Those are not the same thing.

## Try this the next time you speak

Before you explain an idea, ask yourself three questions:

**What is the actual point?**

**What does this audience need in order to understand it?**

**What am I adding because I need them to know how much I know?**

That third question may sting a little. It can also save your audience ten minutes.

Listen for sentences that keep growing after the message has already landed. Notice when you give four examples where one would do. Notice when you answer a question, then keep talking because silence makes you uncomfortable.

The goal is not to become a minimalist speaker.

The goal is to stop burying your strongest ideas under Smart Stuff.

## Make your intelligence easier to use

The smartest person in the room does not need to sound like the smartest person in the room.

Your intelligence should show up in your ability to see what matters, make connections other people miss, and explain difficult things in language people can actually use.

That is a different kind of expertise.

You know more, so choose better. Choose the point. Choose the example. Choose the words that carry the idea without burying it.

Let the audience feel the depth without making them swim through all of it.

Knowledge can be impressive. Clarity makes it useful.

So the next time you feel yourself adding one more explanation, one more example, one more disclaimer, stop and ask:

**Am I adding clarity, or am I adding Smart Stuff?**

Sometimes the smartest thing you can do is let the point stand.

## TL;DR

**Smart Stuff** is what happens when you know so much that you stuff your message with more information than the audience needs. The information may be smart, useful, and completely accurate, but too much of it can bury the point.

Expressive Intelligence™ helps you recognize when you are explaining for the audience and when you are explaining to prove your own credibility. Your expertise should make your message easier to understand, not harder to find.

Start with the point. Give people what they need to understand it. Then have enough confidence to stop.
